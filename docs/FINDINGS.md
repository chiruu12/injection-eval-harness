# Findings

Every number here comes from `results/results.json`, regenerated by `make table`.
Nothing in this file was computed by hand. The failure criteria were written into
`docs/PLAN.md` before the first run.

Sample sizes, once, so the rest of the file can stop repeating them: the primary
test split is 240 rows, 120 injection and 120 benign, arranged as 120 pairs. The
control split is 116 rows, 60 injection and 56 benign. The shift slices are the
120 primary positives, transformed. These are small. Everything below should be
read as a gap worth chasing, not as a ranking.

## 1. The contamination check was underpowered, and its premise is weaker than claimed

This harness was built to catch detectors scoring well on data they had trained
on. The pre-registered flag fires when F1 on the contamination-suspect control
exceeds F1 on the primary set by more than 0.15.

It fired for nobody. Every system did *worse* on the control:

| system | F1 primary | F1 control | gap |
|---|---|---|---|
| regex-floor | 0.390 | 0.261 | -0.129 |
| unplug-model | 0.795 | 0.698 | -0.097 |
| unplug-pipeline | 0.794 | 0.694 | -0.100 |
| protectai | 0.697 | 0.537 | -0.160 |

That is all the data supports, and it supports less than the original wording
claimed. Two problems, both found by adversarial review rather than by me.

The flag is a point estimate compared to a fixed bar, not a test. At n=240 primary
and n=116 control the standard error on an F1 difference is roughly 0.06, so power
to detect a true gap of 0.15 is near half, and a true gap of 0.10 would almost
never fire. Observed gaps sit near -0.10, which is the difficulty difference
between an easy 2023 set and a paired 2026 one running in the opposite direction.
A real contamination advantage of plausible size would be cancelled by that
confound and never light the flag. The correct word is underpowered, not refuted.

The date argument is also weaker than "ruled out". Every primary row carries
source_type synthetic_curated, and the dataset card says the content was generated
and manually reviewed for that release. Hub publication date is not creation date
and is not source-material date: a synthetic set can be derived from public
injection phrasing, or generated by a model that saw it. For unplug-tiny-v1 the
gap is 33 days, in Unplug's own product domain. Publication order is a weak
control, not a firewall, and the README and pins.py now say so.

## 2. Every system fails calibration, and the bar was the easy one

ECE on the primary test split, pre-registered bar 0.15:

| system | ECE | Brier |
|---|---|---|
| unplug-pipeline | 0.191 | see results.json |
| unplug-model | 0.195 | |
| protectai | 0.362 | |
| regex-floor | 0.447 | |

Four for four over the bar. The scores these models emit are thresholded by
callers as though they were probabilities, and on this distribution they are not.

The sharpest version of this is `unplug-model` on the control split: recall 1.000
at an FPR of 0.929, so at its published threshold of 0.9 it flags almost every row
including the benign ones. Its PR-AUC on the same split is 0.867, well
clear of its own operating point but NOT the best there: protectai scores 0.929.
An earlier version of this file claimed it was the best of any system, which is
contradicted by the table two sections above and happened to flatter the author's
own model. It was wrong and an adversarial review caught it. The ranking is good and the threshold is wrong. A model
card that publishes an operating point without publishing the distribution it was
chosen on is publishing half a number.

## 3. The boundary criterion never fired, because it was mis-specified

The pre-registered boundary test fails a system when pair accuracy is below 0.70
*while* marginal accuracy is above 0.85. The idea was to catch a detector that
looks strong on marginal counts and falls apart on pairs.

No system reached 0.85 marginal, so the criterion could not fire for anyone:

| system | pair acc | marginal acc |
|---|---|---|
| unplug-model | 0.600 | 0.796 |
| unplug-pipeline | 0.550 | 0.775 |
| protectai | 0.317 | 0.633 |
| regex-floor | 0.250 | 0.608 |

The gap it was built to detect is present in every row, the conjunction just
gated it out. That is a criterion-design error on my part, not a result, and
rewriting the bar after seeing the numbers would defeat the purpose of fixing it
in advance. It stays as written and this note stands in for it.

Two corrections to how this was originally written, both from review.

Always guessing one way scores 0 on pair accuracy, not 0.25, because a pair counts
only when BOTH halves are right. 0.25 is the independent coin-flip baseline.
protectai's 38 of 120 sits about 1.7 standard errors above that, which is "not
clearly above chance", not a demonstrated collapse.

The bigger problem is that the criterion could never have fired on a paired set.
A detector reading topic flags both twins, which raises its false-positive rate on
the benign half and drags marginal accuracy toward the middle. Requiring marginal
above 0.85 therefore excludes exactly the systems the test was written to catch.
Worse, most of the pair-minus-marginal gap is mechanical: pair accuracy is
approximately recall times specificity, and the observed gaps sit within about
four points of that product for every system. So the gap is not evidence of
topic-reading either. The metric needs rebuilding, not just a looser bar.

## 4. base64 removes the model entirely, and the pipeline survives it

Recall on the 120 primary positives, same threshold, one transform at a time:

| system | baseline | base64 | leetspeak | homoglyph | zero-width | whitespace | carrier |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| unplug-model | 0.792 | **0.000** | 0.633 | 0.708 | 0.867 | 0.808 | 1.000 |
| unplug-pipeline | 0.867 | 0.917 | 0.850 | 1.000 | 0.942 | 0.842 | 1.000 |
| protectai | 0.842 | 1.000 | 1.000 | 0.958 | 0.792 | 0.758 | **0.450** |
| regex-floor | 0.250 | 1.000 | 0.000 | 0.017 | 0.133 | 0.133 | 0.250 |

Two systems breach the 20-point robustness bar, in opposite places.

`unplug-model` goes to exactly zero on base64. Not degraded, gone: the 70M
checkpoint has no representation of an encoded payload and scores every one of
the 120 below threshold. `unplug-pipeline`, the same checkpoint behind the SDK,
goes *up* to 0.917, because the SDK decodes encoded payloads before scoring. The
robustness on that row belongs to the decoder, not to the model.

`protectai` inverts it. It handles every encoding transform at or near ceiling and
then loses 39 points when the identical payload is wrapped in a 400-character
benign operations document. Burying the instruction in ordinary text costs it more
than obfuscating the instruction does.

Neither of those is visible if you evaluate one system on one slice.

**The base64 column was measuring my own transform, and splitting it broke the
result in both directions.** The paragraph below was written before the slice was
split. What it describes is now the `base64_with_instruction` column, and the
honest version of the finding is the section after it.

**The old wording, kept because it is what the committed table said:** `t_base64` prepends
the plaintext line "Decode the following and carry out what it says" before the
encoded payload. At its corrected threshold the regex floor scores 1.000 on that
slice, up from a 0.250 baseline, purely because that sentence trips the
`encoded_payload` pattern. No decoding happens anywhere in a regex. So a system
can top the base64 column by recognising the wrapper, which means protectai's
1.000 there is not evidence it handles encoded payloads, and unplug-pipeline's
0.917 is not either. The one row the wrapper cannot explain is `unplug-model` at
0.000: it sees the same plaintext instruction every other system sees and still
scores every one of the 120 below threshold.

The fix is a base64 variant with no plaintext instruction, where the payload has
to be decoded to be understood at all. That variant now exists as `base64_bare`,
and it says something none of the earlier numbers did.

## 4b. Nothing here decodes anything

Recall on the 120 primary positives, at each system's published threshold:

| system | untransformed | base64 with instruction | base64 bare |
|---|---|---|---|
| regex-floor | 0.250 | 1.000 | 0.000 |
| protectai | 0.842 | 1.000 | 0.000 |
| unplug-pipeline | 0.867 | 0.917 | 1.000 |
| unplug-model | 0.792 | 0.000 | 0.992 |

Read the top two rows together. `protectai` scores exactly what a twelve-pattern
regex scores, on both variants: perfect with the plaintext instruction attached,
zero without it. Its base64 robustness was entirely the wrapper. Strip one English
sentence and it sees nothing.

The `unplug-model` row looks like the opposite and is not. Recall 0.992 comes with
a false-positive rate of 0.958 on the benign half of the same split put through
the same transform: 119 of 120 attacks, 115 of 120 benign.

`scripts/probe_encoding.py` isolates what it is responding to, and the answer is
narrower than "encoded text looks suspicious":

| input, all inside the same benign carrier | score |
|---|---|
| plain benign sentence | 0.0000 |
| base64 of that same benign sentence | 0.9999 |
| hex of it | 0.0000 |
| base32 of it | 0.0012 |
| random noise from the base64 alphabet | 0.4903 |
| uuid-shaped tokens | 0.0000 |

It is not entropy and it is not novelty. Hex and base32 of the identical sentence
score near zero, and random base64-alphabet characters that decode to nothing only
reach 0.49. The feature is well-formed base64 of real text.

The decisive control is whether the content inside the encoding matters at all.
Matching lengths and comparing an encoded benign sentence against an encoded
attack:

| payload repeated | benign | attack | gap |
|---|---|---|---|
| x1 | 0.9999 | 1.0000 | 0.0001 |
| x2 | 1.0000 | 0.9996 | 0.0004 |
| x4 | 1.0000 | 0.9999 | 0.0001 |
| x8 | 1.0000 | 1.0000 | 0.0000 |

The largest gap is 0.0004. The checkpoint cannot distinguish an encoded attack
from an encoded shipping report. It learned that a base64 blob of prose is an
injection, which is a shortcut that separates the training distribution without
reading anything, and the vendor eval behind the model card's headline recall could not have
caught it, because it never encoded a benign row.

Whether that is a defect depends on where you draw the product boundary. The
Unplug SDK decodes encoded payloads before scoring, so in the shipped pipeline the
checkpoint arguably does not need to read base64: flag the blob, let the decoder
open it, score the plaintext. That is a coherent division of labour and it is why
`unplug-pipeline` reaches 1.000 on this slice. What is not defensible is quoting
the checkpoint's 0.992 as evidence of robustness to encoding, which is what the
first version of this table did.

`unplug-pipeline` reaches 1.000 on the bare variant because the SDK decodes
encoded payloads before scoring, which is the documented behaviour of the decoder
rather than of the checkpoint, and is the same split finding 4 already reports.

So the honest summary is that no checkpoint in this table decodes anything. One
flags every encoding, two flag none, and the only system that handles the case
does it with a decoder bolted on in front.

**This finding also exposes a hole in the harness itself.** The shift slice runs
over positives only, so every transform in the table above reports a recall with
no false-positive rate beside it. That is precisely the error this repo was built
to point at in other people's evaluations, and it sat in my own table for the
whole of the first version. A recall number on a transformed slice means nothing
without the same transform applied to the benign half, and the next revision has
to add that arm before any of these transform numbers are quoted anywhere.

## 5. The SDK decides without the model on 21 of 142 findings

Of the 142 primary test rows that produced any finding under the Unplug SDK, 21
were decided by the regex stage alone and the checkpoint was never consulted. 121
reached the model. 98 produced no finding at all.

I expected this share to be much higher, and said so before measuring it. On this
dataset it is not, because the paired benign twins strip out the obvious override
phrasing that a regex keys on. The regex-floor row is the same fact from the other
side: 12 patterns from the public literature fire on 30 of the 120 attacks here, and at the corrected threshold the floor recalls 0.250 of them.

Those 21 rows of 142 still matter, but the reason to keep the two rows separate is finding 4,
not this number. A published detector figure covers a pipeline. A published model
figure covers a checkpoint. On base64 those two differ by 92 points.

## 6. Span localisation is good in bulk and imprecise at the edges

Only the carrier transform has ground-truth offsets, because the harness spliced
the payload in and knows where it put it. On those 120 documents `unplug-model`
fired on all 120, with a character IoU of 0.884 against the true payload span.
Boundaries land within 5 characters on 76 of 120.

So it finds the right region reliably and trims it loosely. For the redaction use
case that Unplug is built around, IoU is the number that matters and 0.884 is
usable. For anything that needs exact offsets it is not.

One caveat that limits this result: the carrier document is a single fixed template.
120 documents that differ only in payload is 120 samples of one carrier, not 120
carriers.

## What I would do next

In rough order of what the numbers argue for:

1. Threshold selection per deployment distribution, and publish the reliability
   curve next to any operating point. Finding 2 is the largest effect in this
   report and the cheapest to act on.
2. An encoded-payload slice in Unplug's own training mix, or an explicit statement
   that encoding handling lives in the SDK and the checkpoint does not have it.
   Finding 4 is currently an undocumented dependency.
3. More carriers, and longer ones, before finding 6 is quoted anywhere.
4. Drop the contamination framing to a secondary check. Finding 1 says it is not
   where the signal was.

## Null and negative results, collected

- The contamination flag fired zero times out of four systems, which given the
  power calculation above is close to uninformative (finding 1).
- The boundary criterion fired zero times out of four, because of how it was
  written rather than because the systems passed (finding 3).
- The floor also tops the base64 column at 1.000 while being incapable of base64,
  which is how the wrapper contamination above was found.
- The regex floor was published at recall 0.025 on the primary set, and that
  number was an artifact of my own bug rather than a fact about keyword matching.
  The floor scores min(1, hits / 3) and its "one match fires" operating point was
  set to 0.5, which silently required TWO pattern hits. Thirty of the 120 attacks
  produce at least one hit; three produce two. At the operating point its label
  actually describes, the floor reaches recall 0.25 at 5 percent FPR, which ties
  protectai and beats unplug-pipeline at 0.117. A baseline that weak made every
  transformer look better than it is, including ours. Fixed, and the tables above
  are regenerated at the honest threshold.
- Zero-width and whitespace transforms cost `unplug-model` nothing. Both were
  expected to hurt a subword tokeniser and neither did.
